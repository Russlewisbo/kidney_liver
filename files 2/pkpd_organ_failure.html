Welcome and thanks for the invitation. Over the next 30 minutes I want to make one argument: that the way we adjust antimicrobials in organ failure — pulling a number out of a label table — is at odds with what we now know about the pharmacokinetics of these patients. I’ll show you the evidence, and end with a practical algorithm you can use tomorrow.

Organ failure is the rule, not the exception, in patients who most need effective antimicrobials

AKI in septic ICU patients ≈ 50%. Cirrhosis carries ICU mortality > 50% when infection coexists. We dose these patients every day — usually with assumptions that don’t apply.

The framing point: this isn’t a niche population. Half of septic ICU patients develop AKI; cirrhotic patients with bacteremia have ICU mortality north of 50%. If our dosing is wrong in these patients, we are dosing wrong in a large fraction of the patients who most need correct dosing. Empirical “renal and hepatic dose adjustment” tables systematically miss the target in critically ill patients

This is the rhetorical hook. Label-recommended adjustment tables were derived mostly from stable outpatients with measured GFR. When you take a real ICU cohort and measure exposure on those doses, a striking fraction sit outside the PK/PD target — often below. The DALI study showed roughly 20% of β-lactam patients did not even reach 50% fT>MIC on standard regimens. Replace placeholder with a DALI-style scatter or a Roberts/Lipman summary figure.

PK/PD framework

Antibiotic efficacy is governed by three PK/PD indices that respond differently to organ dysfunction

Three classes of indices. Concentration-dependent (aminoglycosides, Cmax/MIC), exposure-dependent (vancomycin, fluoroquinolones, fAUC/MIC), and time-dependent (β-lactams, fT>MIC). The reason these distinctions matter for organ failure is that they respond differently to changes in clearance. A β-lactam in a patient with low clearance suddenly looks great by fT>MIC and terrible by toxicity; a fluoroquinolone in ARC looks great by Cmax and terrible by AUC. Same patient, different drug, opposite answer. This is the slide I’ll come back to throughout the talk.

Only the unbound fraction of drug crosses membranes and binds its target

Total-drug concentrations are a convenience measurement. Free drug is the pharmacology.

This is the conceptual foundation for slides 14 and 15. Every PK/PD target in the literature should technically be expressed as free drug. For drugs with low protein binding (<30%), the distinction barely matters. For drugs with high binding — ceftriaxone 95%, ertapenem 95%, flucloxacillin 95%, teicoplanin 90% — small changes in binding mean large changes in free fraction, and total-drug TDM becomes unreliable. We’ll quantify this in the protein-binding section.

Kidney failure

Renal dysfunction prolongs the half-life of renally cleared antibiotics in proportion to lost GFR

Standard teaching, but the visual reinforces the heterogeneity. Note the fan-out at low CrCl — half-lives separate by factors of 5 to 10. This is also where label-recommended doses diverge most. Creatinine-based GFR estimates are unreliable in the critically ill and frequently misclassify clearance

Limits of agreement span > 100 mL/min. Systematic underestimation in ARC. Cockcroft-Gault was never meant for the unstable patient.

This is one of the two figures I want you to take a photo of. The bias is modest in the middle range and a bedside clinician might call it acceptable. But look at the limits of agreement — over 100 mL/min wide. That means if I hand you a CG-estimated CrCl of 80, the true value is somewhere between 30 and 130. You cannot dose meropenem or vancomycin off that number with any confidence. The systematic underestimation in the ARC zone — right side, red shading — is the second problem: every estimate underestimates ARC, so we systematically underdose the patients with the highest clearance. Baptista, Udy, and others have shown this repeatedly. Solution is urinary 8h CrCl when the patient is catheterized — which is most of the ICU.

Augmented renal clearance produces sub-therapeutic exposure in up to half of younger septic patients

ARC, defined as measured CrCl > 130 mL/min/1.73m², is reported in 30–65% of septic ICU patients under 50, hyperdynamic, post-trauma, or burns. The histogram I’ll put here should show the mode of the population sitting in the ARC zone, which makes the point viscerally. The inset Monte Carlo result — percentage attaining fT>MIC on standard meropenem dosing in ARC patients — typically sits below 50%. The actionable point: ARC is the opposite problem to renal failure, but it is much more common in the ICU and we routinely miss it.

Drug removal during CRRT depends on modality, effluent rate, sieving coefficient, and protein binding — not on the dialyzer alone

CLCRRT ≈ Qeffluent × Sc. Highly protein-bound or low-Sc drugs are barely removed; meropenem, β-lactams, vancomycin: substantial removal at modern effluent rates.

The two things to internalize here. First, modern CRRT effluent rates (25-35 mL/kg/h, often higher than prescribed because of downtime) result in clearances of 25-50 mL/min for highly diffusible drugs. That is a non-trivial fraction of normal renal clearance. Second, the sieving coefficient drives who actually gets cleared. Meropenem ~1.0, ceftriaxone ~0.2, daptomycin ~0.15. So a vague instruction to “halve the dose for CRRT” can be wrong by a factor of ten. The Pea/Roberts equations let you do this estimate at the bedside. I’ll keep the equation small and make the modality schematic the focus.

Loading doses depend on volume of distribution and must not be reduced for renal dysfunction

Loading dose is a function of Vd, not of CL. Reducing it delays target attainment by 1–3 half-lives.

This is the one bit of teaching that most undergraduate pharmacy curricula get wrong and most clinicians inherit. The loading dose is determined by the desired concentration and the volume of distribution: Dload = Ctarget × Vd. Renal dysfunction does not shrink Vd — if anything it expands it via fluid overload, capillary leak, hypoalbuminemia. Reducing the loading dose for “renal failure” simply delays target attainment by one to three half-lives — which in a septic patient is exactly when target attainment matters most. I’ll show two simulated curves: full loading dose hitting target within 4 hours, reduced loading dose not crossing target for 24+ hours.

Liver failure

Cirrhosis alters drug exposure through four parallel mechanisms that do not move together

A 2×2 grid: reduced CYP/phase-II activity, expanded Vd (ascites/edema), hypoalbuminemia, biliary stasis. Example drugs in each quadrant — tigecycline (metabolism), ceftriaxone (binding + biliary), moxifloxacin (metabolism + QT), micafungin (biliary, but largely safe). The point of showing them as parallel mechanisms is that they do not correlate — a patient can have normal CYP function and severe hypoalbuminemia, or vice versa, and the dosing implication is different. This is why a single Child-Pugh class is too coarse to drive dosing.

Child-Pugh class predicts mortality better than it predicts pharmacokinetics

Within each Child-Pugh class, drug clearance spans 3–10× — far wider than between classes.

Child-Pugh was derived as a prognostic score for surgical mortality. It correlates with PK on average, but the within-class variability is enormous. Voriconazole clearance in Child-Pugh B patients spans about an order of magnitude. Tigecycline AUC roughly doubles between A and C, but with considerable overlap. So Child-Pugh is a starting point — it tells you that adjustment is needed and which direction — but it cannot tell you how much. For drugs with TDM (voriconazole, isavuconazole), measure. For drugs without, expect wide variability and consider conservative starting doses with early clinical reassessment.

Hepatorenal syndrome compounds elimination defects and converts a “hepatic” drug problem into a renal one

The clinical trap. HRS patients have markedly reduced GFR — often below 30 mL/min — but their serum creatinine looks deceptively reasonable because of reduced muscle mass and reduced hepatic creatine synthesis. So the estimated CrCl from Cockcroft-Gault overestimates real clearance by a factor of two or more. If you reach for a renally cleared antibiotic — aminoglycoside, vancomycin, polymyxin — in a cirrhotic patient with HRS, the standard renal adjustment tables will systematically overdose. Cystatin C is more reliable in this population, or measured CrCl when possible.

Protein binding

Hypoalbuminemia raises the free fraction of highly bound antibiotics, but also accelerates their clearance

For ceftriaxone, ertapenem, flucloxacillin, teicoplanin: free AUC may be preserved despite hypoalbuminemia. Reflexive dose escalation is often wrong.

This is the most counter-intuitive slide in the talk. When albumin falls, the free fraction of a highly bound drug rises — that part is obvious. What is less obvious is that for many drugs the total clearance also rises, because clearance of the unbound fraction is unchanged but a larger proportion is unbound and available for elimination. The net effect on free AUC is often near-zero — sometimes slightly higher, sometimes lower. So the right answer to “low albumin, what do I do with the ceftriaxone dose” is usually “nothing” — unless the patient is also volume-resuscitated, in which case Vd is the larger driver. The point is that the answer requires thinking, not a reflex.

For highly protein-bound antibiotics, total-drug TDM can mislead and free-drug measurement matters

The clinical extension. If you do measure ceftriaxone or flucloxacillin TDM in a hypoalbuminemic patient, the total concentration can look reassuringly in-range while the free concentration sits below MIC. This has been documented in critically ill flucloxacillin patients with endocarditis. Practical implication: when you have access to free-drug measurement and the patient has albumin <25 g/L and is on a highly bound antibiotic, measure the free fraction. When you don’t have access, assume the free fraction is higher than predicted by population PK and lean on clinical response.

Optimization

Extended and continuous infusion of β-lactams achieves PK/PD targets that intermittent dosing cannot in patients with altered Vd

At MIC 4–8 mg/L — the breakpoint zone — intermittent dosing fails the 90% PTA target while extended and continuous infusion clear it.

This is the second figure I want you to take a photo of. Monte Carlo simulation, 5000 virtual ICU patients with realistic BSV on CL and Vd, target 50% fT>MIC. At low MIC, everything works. At MIC ≥4 — which includes the EUCAST breakpoint for Pseudomonas at 2 — intermittent dosing falls below the 90% PTA target while extended and continuous infusion remain above. The difference between extended and continuous is modest; the difference from intermittent is large. This is the strongest practical argument for prolonged infusion. The recent BLING-III and MERCY trials have given us large RCT data supporting this; while not unanimous, the PK/PD case is robust.

Bayesian model-informed precision dosing translates measured concentrations into individualized regimens at the bedside

MIPD is the operational answer to everything we’ve said so far. You start with a population prior, you measure a concentration, the software computes a posterior estimate of the patient’s individual CL and Vd, and recommends a next dose to hit the PD target. Tools: InsightRx, DoseMeRx, BestDose, the open-source nlmixr2 + brms stack. The barrier is not the math — it is workflow integration with the lab and the pharmacy. Centers that have made this work (Antwerp, Liverpool, Rotterdam, ours in Padova) show measurable improvement in target attainment. I’ll show one anonymized case from our practice — beta-lactam dose individualization on day 2 of a CRRT patient.

A practical bedside algorithm anchors dosing in five questions

The synthesis slide. Five questions in order. First: what is the free fraction? Highly bound drug + low albumin = use free-drug targets. Second: is Vd altered? Sepsis, capillary leak, CRRT — give a full loading dose. Third: what is the clearance? Renal, hepatic, mixed, and the diagnostic pathway differs. Fourth: match maintenance to the PK/PD index — fT>MIC means prolonged infusion, AUC/MIC means Bayesian dosing, Cmax/MIC means extended intervals. Fifth: trigger TDM and MIPD on day 1 or 2, and re-sample after any meaningful change in organ function. If you take one image home, take this one.

Take-home

Optimal antimicrobial dosing in organ failure requires individualization, not adjustment tables

Free-drug targets matter when albumin is low and binding is high.
Loading doses are governed by Vd — never reduce them for organ failure.
Maintenance doses must match the PK/PD index and the measured clearance.
Extended or continuous infusion of β-lactams should be the default in the ICU.
TDM + Bayesian MIPD is the operational answer to all of the above.

Five things to take home. The talk has been one extended argument that dose-adjustment tables are necessary but not sufficient in critically ill patients with organ failure. Free-drug targets, full loading doses, maintenance-dose matching to the PK/PD index, prolonged infusion, and TDM with Bayesian dosing are the levers we have. None are exotic; all are underused.
